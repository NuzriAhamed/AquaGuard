3.1.5 End-to-End Threat Classification Observability 
The presence of a working system architecture does not establish the end -to-end confusion 
behavior of the FSM. An actual monitoring study must compare predicted alert states against 
annotations on time -aligned scenes, not simply compare detector class names against ground 
truth. A single video clip might contain several person –animal pairs with different centroids; a 
model error on the nearest pair could matter more than an error on a distant pair. This motivates a 
dedicated threat-state evaluation dataset. 
For each annotated frame, the evaluator would record true animal and human bounding boxes, the 
correct minimum image -plane distance under a documented coordinate convention and the 
derived reference state. Predicted state and transition timestamps can then be compared with the 
reference. A four -by-four state confusion matrix, missed -critical percentage, false -critical

percentage and mean transition delay are more directly related to system purpose than animal -
species mAP alone. None of these measurements can be derived from the currently provided data. 
An additional test should examine threshold stability. Two video sequences might contain the 
same subjects with different camera shake or tracking noise; centroid estimates may move above 
and below 220 pixels even if the true subjects are not changing their distance significantly. A 
stable system should have a documented behavior under this condition. The provided FSM does 
not specify such a stabilization policy, so its raw behavior should be measured first as the baseline

3.2 Research Findings 
3.2.1 Observed Training Convergence 
The most direct numerical finding is the large improvement between the initial and final reported 
detector checkpoints. Box, class and DFL losses all decline, and precision, recall and mAP@0.5 
all rise. These trends are mutually consistent with successful optimization during the reported run. 
Their interpretive boundary is that the complete training logs and split metadata are unavailable. 
The figures show strong reported checkpoint performance, but they cannot establish external 
validity in an environment not represented by the source experiment. 
A second finding is the apparent performance plateau in the provided late observations. Epochs 
57 and 100 share the same supplied precision, recall and mAP@0.5 values despite small 
continuing decreases in the loss components. This suggests that additional training beyond the 
earlier reported checkpoint may yield limited measured benefit under the same evaluation 
conditions. A full experiment would compare the checkpoints on an independent set and verify 
whether any performance difference is meaningful in relation to model runtime cost. 
3.2.2 Class-Specific Strengths and Weaknesses 
The diagonal entries in the supplied matrix are generally high for the seven foreground categories, 
with cow at 0.90 and elephant/person at 0.95. The background row is lower at 0.82. These 
patterns suggest that some of the reported errors involve distinguishing visually related animal 
classes and separating foreground detections from background. They indicate useful areas for 
targeted annotation review, but they do not identify whether errors originate from image 
resolution, poor lighting, label ambiguity or a particular preprocessing decision. 
The significance of an error depends on the application layer. Confusing cow with buffalo could 
leave the animal -presence branch active and therefore preserve the broad threat state, while 
missing a human may suppress CAUTION or CRITICAL entirely. It is therefore insufficient to 
optimize only average species recognition. For field -oriented evaluation, class confusion should

be mapped to the likelihood of an incorrect alert state and the consequence of that alert error. This 
requires paired detector and FSM records. 
3.2.3 Importance of Explicit Spatial Logic 
A central design finding is that the four -level alert depends on a small set of understandable 
conditions: animal presence, human presence and d_min at a single boundary. This makes its 
decision process auditable. An evaluator can reconstruct why a frame was labeled CRITICAL by 
checking that at least one animal and person were valid and that their nearest centroids were no 
farther apart than 220 pixels. Unlike a black-box behavior classifier, the rule can be verified using 
synthetic geometric inputs before introducing detector uncertainty. 
The transparency of the rule is also its limitation. A person visually close to an animal in the same 
image may be separated in depth, and the same physical encounter may project to a very different 
pixel distance from another camera. The design’s output is consequently best described as an 
image-space proximity alert . Calibrating physical risk would require additional observational 
information and expert validation that are not part of the supplied data. 
3.2.4 Confidence and Temporal Stability 
The reported 80% confidence rule gives the tracking architecture a named selection criterion, but 
its exact semantics are underspecified. This prevents a strong conclusion about whether identity 
locking reduces false alarms, improves precision or causes missed brief encounters. A rigorous 
experiment should compare the system with and without the identity -lock policy, keeping 
detector weights and test videos fixed. The outcome measures should include track ID switches, 
false state transitions, missed critical events and latency. 
Temporal stability is especially relevant for near -threshold conditions. A single -frame detector 
can produce isolated false positives, whereas persistent tracks may remove some noise. At the 
same time, retaining an outdated track can create a false proximity pairing. This is a classic 
balance between responsiveness and stability that must be tested against actual video sequences. 
The current architecture provides the right conceptual components for such work, but the supplied 
numerical results do not quantify their performance. 
3.3 Discussions 
3.3.1 Relationship Between Detector Metrics and Practical Safety 
The detector’s final reported precision of 0.914, recall of 0.885 and mAP@0.5 of 0.927 are 
promising values in the context of the supplied experiment. However, an encounter alert is 
formed only after several transformations: image decoding, two model outputs, tracking, centroid 
calculation, minimum-pair selection, state assignment and notification delivery. A failure at any 
--- PAGE 45 ---
Wildlife Threat Detection • Individual Research Report 
36 
later stage can weaken end -to-end performance without changing the model -level mAP. This is 
why the proposed testing matrix deliberately evaluates every stage rather than treating the 
detector as the entire product. 
A monitoring system must also distinguish error severity. Some wrong species labels may have 
little effect on the basic proximity rule, while a missed human near an animal is much more 
important to the desired warning outcome. A useful future evaluation would assign severity 
categories to state -level false negatives and false positives, then report them separately. The 
policy should be established with domain experts, not chosen solely to make the numeric results 
look favorable. 
3.3.2 Comparison with Existing Approaches 
Compared with manual review of camera footage, an automated pipeline offers the possibility of 
continuous screening and prompt event prioritization. It may reduce the effort of finding 
candidate animal –human encounters in long recordings. Manual review remains important for 
verifying unexpected model outputs and interpreting the ecological meaning of an event. The 
findings of camera -trap research reinforce the need for human involvement where visual 
conditions or small targets challenge automated systems [2]. 
Compared with single -frame species detection, the proposed architecture adds temporal identity 
information and explicit pairwise proximity. Compared with tracking alone, it defines event 
semantics through the FSM. Compared with an unconstrained machine -learned threat classifier, 
the geometric threshold is easier to explain and regression -test. These comparisons describe 
conceptual design properties; no controlled baseline benchmarks were provided that would justify 
a claim of quantitatively superior end-to-end performance. 
3.3.3 Methodological Limitations 
The first limitation is incomplete experimental provenance. The supplied curves and matrix are 
numeric results, but the original dataset, source notebook, training configuration and validation 
split are not available. The thesis therefore cannot determine whether checkpoint metrics were 
generated on training, validation or held -out test data, or whether repeated video frames caused 
leakage across partitions. These questions must be resolved before a final research evaluation is 
considered reproducible. 
The second limitation is the absence of raw confusion counts and class supports. This prevents 
calculation of support -weighted accuracy, confidence intervals or class -specific sample sizes. A 
class with few examples can achieve a deceptively high normalized diagonal rate. Without 
support counts, readers cannot judge the statistical reliability of the displayed 0.90 –0.95 
--- PAGE 46 ---

37 
foreground values. The matrix is still informative for visualizing the reported distribution, but its 
statistical meaning is limited. 
The third limitation is the measurement model. Euclidean centroid distance is defined in pixels 
and is not physically calibrated. Bounding box centroids may also be unstable under partial 
occlusion; the center of a visible box can move when an animal turns or is partly hidden, even if 
the animal’s physical position changes little. The 220 -pixel threshold therefore needs camera -
specific validation and should not be marketed as a safe real-world separation. 
The fourth limitation is unverified temporal tracking. The 80% identity -lock rule is specified, but 
details of track confirmation, re -identification and deletion were not supplied. Since threat states 
depend on tracks, these missing details directly affect event stability. The fifth limitation is absent 
end-to-end operational evidence: no alert delivery latency, buzzer measurement, throughput 
benchmark, multi-camera trial, memory profile or user acceptance results were supplied. 
The sixth limitation concerns background and human handling in the combined confusion matrix. 
The architecture explicitly uses two models, but the normalization procedure for the combined 
matrix is not described. If the matrix combines model streams, its construction needs careful 
explanation to avoid mistaking it for a native single -model classification confusion matrix. The 
analysis preserves the provided row/column labels while openly identifying this uncertainty. 
The seventh limitation is ecological scope. A general animal –human proximity rule does not 
distinguish protective herd behavior, grazing, fleeing, curiosity or aggression. It also treats a cow 
and a leopard similarly for the purpose of basic state selection. Such a rule can support situational 
awareness, but it cannot substitute for professional risk assessment. Responsible deployment 
needs local protocols, stakeholder participation and periodic review [1].

3.3.4 Future Enhancements and Validation 
The first priority is to collect a fully versioned set of annotated videos reflecting the intended 
camera deployments. Separate videos should include each animal class, mixed animal –human 
scenes, overlapping targets and common environmental disturbances. Every video should carry 
metadata such as device, frame size, collection conditions and permission status. These materials 
would support both detection evaluation and independent assessment of the proximity state 
machine. 
The second priority is to evaluate tracking and event integrity. Stable IDs, the use of the 80% lock 
rule, response to temporary occlusion and the absence of stale pairs should be inspected with test 
traces. The third priority is to measure system timing and resilience under actual hardware loads. 
A reported mAP score alone cannot demonstrate that the alert reaches a user in time; capture -to-
alert latency and missed event rates must be measured directly. 
The fourth priority is to study distance normalization and calibration. Candidate approaches 
include per -camera threshold configuration, normalization by image dimensions, perspective -
specific scaling or physical calibration where feasible. Each alternative should be compared on 
the same annotated encounter videos. The fifth priority is operator -centered validation: 
participants should understand state meanings, receive manageable alert volumes and know that 
the system does not infer animal intent. 
Only after these steps would it be appropriate to report a field -level claim such as a quantified 
critical-event recall or a maximum alert delay. Those claims require external validation records 
and cannot be backfilled from the supplied model training curves. This position does not diminish 
the design contribution; rather, it distinguishes an interpretable research prototype from a 
validated safety product.
